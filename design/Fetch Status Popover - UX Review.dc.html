1 Findings — four gates stand between the card and the screen

The remote‑activity card is good, complete, and almost never seen. It is summoned by hover, withheld for the operation's first 1.2 s, and torn down the instant the operation ends — so the only fetch it can describe is one slow enough to outlast the gate and under a pointer that happens to be resting on the right control. Specimens below are drawn at shipped size from styles/app.css and tokens.css, on contrived repository names.

2 The proposal — the click opens it, the outcome closes it

Pressing Fetch, Pull or Push pins the card open immediately, with no age gate and no hover requirement. It stays for the whole operation, then keeps going: it becomes the receipt. A success drains a countdown rail and leaves; a failure stands until dismissed. One surface answers “what is it doing?” and “what did it do?”, in the one place the user is already looking.

3 The four calls inside it

The lifecycle above is settled. These are the decisions it forces, each with a recommendation and the reason — three of them are cheap to change later, one of them decides how much of the app this touches.

3aWhere does the outcome come from?
Chosen A — the header already has it

WorktreeHeader awaits the Result for every fetch, pull and push it dispatches, and already turns it into the chip flash: fast‑forwarded, pulled · changes reapplied, pulled · resolve stash conflicts, push denied. That richness exists nowhere else. Hand it to the popover as an explicit settled input instead of deriving the card’s life from activity !== null.

No IPC change, no new main‑process state, no change to what remote:activity means. The blast radius is the popover hook and the header that owns it.

✓Recommended — smallest change that delivers the whole ask
Deferred B — main emits a terminal record

Give RemoteActivity an outcome and let finished records linger a few seconds. Every surface gets receipts for free, including operations started from the sidebar, a second window, or bulk sync — the cases A leaves showing nothing, exactly as today.

It also changes what “live activities” means for every consumer, and the elsewhere‑toast would start showing receipts for repositories you are not looking at, which is a separate design question with its own answer.

·Not now — right shape, wrong scope for this change
3bDoes hover‑to‑open survive?
Yes, un‑pinned, and the age gate goes

The click path is the one the user asked for, and it makes the whole stationary‑pointer problem moot: the card is already open before any mouseenter can fire, so the enter finds it open and does nothing. The 1.2 s gate exists only to suppress a card nobody asked for — and a click is an ask. It goes.

Hover still earns a card in the one case a click cannot cover: an operation against this checkout that someone else started — the sidebar’s refresh, bulk sync, a second window. That card is transient (leaves with the pointer) and never grows a rail, because there is no settle to report. All of WHERE_THE_USER_IS, the re‑arm on record arrival and the Tab handoff stay exactly as they are.

✓Keep — two openings, one card, one dismissal rule
3cHow long is the rail?
4 s you asked for 3 or 5

3 s is short for a two‑line outcome with a diffstat under it; 5 s is long enough that a card sitting over the graph starts to feel like something you have to wait out. 4 s reads one sentence plus a glance at the output with room to spare, and it is comfortably under the 9 s the error toasts use — those appear unasked and have to survive being ignored; this one you just summoned and are looking straight at.

Cheap to move: one constant. Try 2d on both and say which.

3dThe rail under reduced motion, and the WCAG line
1

A drained rail is an animation, and app.css kills all of them

The blanket prefers-reduced-motion rule at the top of the file is animation: none !important. The toast’s countdown already loses to it — the bar sits full while the toast leaves anyway. Copying that here would leave a success card with a full green rail that vanishes without warning.

✓

Step it from the clock the card already runs

The card ticks once a second while visible (useSecondsClock). Under reduced motion, set the rail’s scaleX from that tick instead of from a keyframe: four discrete steps, no smooth motion, and the information survives. Same timer either way — only the drawing changes.

✓

SC 2.2.1 is satisfied by pause, not by length

Pointer pauses it, a click or focus holds it outright, and ✕ is always there. That is the “Timing Adjustable” escape hatch met three ways — which is also why the rail may be short.

?

The settled card announces once

Running stays silent (the sync chip is the live region and already says “Fetching updates…”). The settle is a single announce() of the outcome line — one utterance, not a counter, so it can be a live region without the problem the card was kept out of one for.

4 “What was all that flashing text telling me?”

Shipped, used, and the verdict from use: the card shows now instead of flashing, but what it shows during an operation is unreadable — text that replaces itself faster than it can be read, a box that resizes while you read it, and then one word at the end. The card was designed to narrate a slow operation. This turn is about the fast one, which is nearly all of them.

4aEvery element is replaced, and a fast pull replaces all of them five times
1

Phase changes bypass the throttle

Output is debounced to 400 ms, but every phase transition calls publish(true) — immediate. A pull walks prepare → fetch → fast_forward → reapply → refresh, so a 620 ms pull lands five full redraws in six tenths of a second.

2

The meter mounts and unmounts

The progress block is drawn only while progress !== null, so it appears for the transfer phase and vanishes for the others. The card changes height under the pointer — twice per pull, minimum.

3

The tail rewrites its own last line

Git’s progress output is \r-rewritten — designed to be transient in a terminal. The record reproduces that faithfully, so the bottom line of the block flickers at Git’s rate while the block itself grows from nothing to its 108 px cap.

4

And then it is all thrown away

settle replaces every one of those elements with a single sentence. Whatever you managed to read had no bearing on what you are left looking at, which is what makes the churn feel like it was hiding something.

one pull, 620ms prepare fetch ff reapply
card redraws 5 immediate · 1 settle — ≈100ms apart
readable? nothing survives long enough
0200ms400ms620ms

The diagnosis is not “too fast”. It is that everything the card draws is replaced rather than accumulated — correct for a five-minute fetch where each phase lasts long enough to read, and exactly wrong below a second, where the narration plays out before the first frame is read.

4cFive rules, and what each one costs
RuleNeedsWhy
Append, never rewrite renderer A row’s text is written once; only its marker moves ●→✓. Text under the eye never moves, which is the whole of “readable”.
Output behind a disclosure renderer Git’s \r progress is evidence for a failure, not narration for a success. Collapsing it removes the flicker and the 0→108 px growth in one move.
No row for a phase that did nothing renderer A pull with nothing to stash must not draw “Preparing local changes”. Most fetches then settle to a single line, which is the honest amount.
Under ≈1 s, no running card at all renderer Click straight to the receipt. Most of the churn is a play-by-play of something that finished before the first frame landed. The pin still happens at the click — only the narration waits.
Rows carry numbers main remote:fetch and remote:push return null; remote:pull returns three booleans. pullBranch holds the commit range either side of the merge and discards it. Without this the rows are honest but wordless — checkmarks, no counts.

The first four ship without touching main, and they are what kills the churn. The fifth is what turns “Fast-forwarded ✓” into “Fast-forwarded · 12 commits”, and it is the same protocol widening card 3a deferred — a much smaller version of it, since the commands need only return what they did rather than emit terminal records.
